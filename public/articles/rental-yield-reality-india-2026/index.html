Rental Yield Reality — Why Indian Property Pays 1–2%

A ₹2 crore Mumbai flat rented at ₹50,000 a month delivers ₹6 lakh a year — a gross yield of 3.0%. After property tax, society maintenance, vacancy, brokerage, repairs, insurance, and the 30% Section 24 standard deduction, the after-tax net is closer to 0.5–1%. This isn't a calculation error. Indian residential property is, structurally, one of the world's lowest-yielding real estate markets. Here's why — and when it still makes sense to buy.

The headline numbers (and why they're misleading)

The most-quoted statistic about Indian property is the headline rental yield: "Mumbai 3%, Bangalore 3.5%." These are gross yields — annual rent divided by property value — and they overstate the actual cash return to an investor by a factor of two to three.

Three layers of cost stand between the gross yield and what actually lands in your bank account:

  1. Operating costs. Property tax, society maintenance, vacancy, brokerage on letting, repair reserve, insurance. These typically deduct 30-50% of gross rental income.
  2. Income tax. Rental income is taxed under "Income from House Property" at your slab rate, with a flat 30% Section 24 standard deduction. For most landlords, this further reduces the take-home by 15-25%.
  3. Capital opportunity cost. The down payment + EMI principal you've parked in the property could have earned 7-12% elsewhere. This isn't a deduction from yield but it changes the comparison.
The honest framing A property advertised at "3.5% gross yield" typically delivers 1.5-2% net pre-tax and 0.5-1.5% after-tax cash yield to the owner. This is the number that matters for comparison against FDs, debt MFs, and equity MFs.

The gross-to-net-to-after-tax bridge

Six operating cost items eat the gap between gross and net rental yield. Here's the typical breakdown for a Tier-1 city property:

  • Property tax to municipal corporation. 0.3–0.5% of property value per year. For a ₹1 crore flat: ₹30K–₹50K. Paid by the owner.
  • Society maintenance / association charges. ₹2,000–₹8,000 per month depending on amenities (lift, security, club, pool). For an established Mumbai building: ₹50K–₹70K/year is typical.
  • Vacancy adjustment. Assume 1 month per year between tenants — even in high-demand neighbourhoods. That's 8.3% of annual rent lost to vacancy.
  • Brokerage on letting. Most Indian residential leases are 11-month leave-and-license agreements. Each renewal cycle typically costs 1 month's rent in broker fees. Over a year, that's 8.3% of annual rent.
  • Repair and renovation reserve. 0.5% of property value per year for major repairs (paint, fittings, AC service, plumbing). For a ₹1Cr flat: ₹50K/year.
  • Property insurance. ₹3,000–₹6,000/year. Cheap but often overlooked.

The income tax math on top of operating costs:

How rental income is taxed in India Start with Gross Annual Value (higher of actual rent and municipal valuation). Deduct municipal taxes paid → Net Annual Value (NAV). Apply flat 30% Section 24 standard deduction. If the property has a home loan, the interest is fully deductible for a let-out property (no cap, unlike the ₹2L cap on self-occupied). The remainder is added to total income and taxed at your slab — 5%, 20%, or 30% plus 4% cess plus surcharge if applicable.

A flat advertised as a "3% gross yield" delivers 0.71% to the owner after the full cost bridge. The same ₹2 crore in a 7% FD would deliver ₹14 lakh/year pre-tax and ~₹9.8 lakh/year after 30% tax — roughly 7× the rental property's cash flow.

The capital appreciation argument The reason buyers tolerate this dismal rental cash flow is the capital appreciation layer. A Mumbai property appreciating at 6-8% per year adds ₹12-16 lakh/year in paper wealth. Add the ₹1.4L rental cash flow and total annual return reaches 7-9%, which is competitive with debt funds. The trade-off: appreciation is paper-only until sale, illiquid, and not guaranteed (recent stretches in Mumbai have seen 0% appreciation for 3-5 years).

Why yields are structurally low

Three forces keep Indian rental yields persistently low compared to global markets. None are likely to change in 2026.

1. Cultural ownership preference

Indians want to OWN homes. Roughly 87% of urban Indian households own (versus rent) the home they live in, one of the highest rates globally. This demand pushes purchase prices up faster than rental demand, compressing yields. The "rentvesting" model common in the US, UK, and Australia — rent your home, invest the down payment in equity — is rare in India.

2. Capital appreciation expectation

Buyers explicitly accept low rental yields because they expect 6-9% annual appreciation. This is a self-fulfilling prophecy: high-priced properties yield low rents, but buyers keep buying because they expect prices to rise further. The cycle continues as long as appreciation expectations hold up.

3. Tenant-favorable rent control

Many state Rent Control Acts (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal) cap rent escalation and make eviction difficult. This protects existing tenants but discourages owners from listing properties at fair market rates and limits how fast rents can grow. The Maharashtra Rent Act of 1999 in particular keeps thousands of South Mumbai flats at ₹500-2000/month rents on properties worth ₹5-10 crore today.

Global comparison for context: US residential 6-8% gross yields, UK 5-7%, Singapore 3-4%, Tokyo 4-5%. Indian metros at 2-3% are among the lowest in any major market — reflecting how much price growth has outrun rent growth over the last 15 years.

When residential property still wins

Despite the low yield, there are specific cases where Indian residential property is a defensible investment. None of them rely on "rent will go up" — all depend on context that isn't just yield-based.

  1. Idle cash that would otherwise sit in savings. A 4% savings account return is genuinely worse than property's 7% blended. If equity isn't on the table for the investor (risk aversion, near retirement), property at 7% is a real upgrade.
  2. Future retirement use. Buying in a Tier-2 city you intend to retire to in 10 years means you've locked in a future home at today's prices. Imputed rental yield to yourself (no rent in retirement) is your slab rate × your future rent expectation — easily worth 4-5%.
  3. Tier-2 cities with 4-5% gross yields. Coimbatore, Indore, Jaipur, Lucknow, and similar markets offer ~50% better yields than metros at much lower entry prices. A ₹50L Tier-2 property at 4.5% gross delivers ~2.5% after-tax — competitive on a smaller capital base.
  4. Forced-savings discipline. Some investors genuinely don't save when not on a fixed schedule. A home loan EMI forces saving via principal repayment. Behaviourally this matters even if mathematically inferior.
  5. Leverage in a rising market. 4-5× leverage on a 20% down payment, in a market with 7%+ appreciation, can generate 25-35% returns on equity. The risk: in a falling or flat market, the same leverage amplifies losses.
  6. Diversification anchor. A house provides a physical, illiquid, non-correlated asset alongside an equity-heavy portfolio. Some investors value this even at lower expected return.

The commercial / Tier-2 alternative

If yield is genuinely the priority — say you want passive income from real estate — residential metro property is the wrong vehicle. Two better options:

Commercial property (office, retail, warehousing)

Tier-1 commercial typically yields 6-8% gross and 3.5-4.5% after-tax. Tenants are corporates with stronger covenants. Lease terms are 3-9 years with built-in escalation (5-7% every 3 years). Property tax and maintenance are often passed to the tenant in commercial agreements. The downside: minimum ticket size is typically ₹2-5 crore, vacancy risk is higher and longer-lasting (4-8 months between tenants is common), and resale liquidity is much weaker than residential.

REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts)

Embassy REIT, Mindspace REIT, Brookfield REIT and Nexus Select Trust collectively give Indian retail investors commercial real estate exposure with daily liquidity, professional management, and SEBI regulation. Current distribution yields are 5.5-7% and total returns (distribution + price) have annualised at 8-11% since listing. Ticket size is ~₹300-500 per unit. For most investors seeking real-estate yield, REITs are a more sensible structure than direct commercial property ownership.

Tier-2 residential

If you specifically want direct residential ownership, look at Tier-2 cities (Indore, Lucknow, Coimbatore, Jaipur, Nagpur, Surat) where gross yields are 4-5% and entry tickets are ₹30-80 lakh. After-tax yields of 2-2.5% are still modest but materially better than Tier-1 metros, and appreciation is often comparable since Tier-2 cities are mid-cycle in their growth trajectory.
